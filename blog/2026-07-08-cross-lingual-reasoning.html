Reasoning efficiency

Most models won't think in the language you ask for

I set out to test whether reasoning in a cheaper language buys the same accuracy for fewer tokens. On hard problems it does not, and the reason has less to do with language than with how the evaluation is scored.

Advaith Appajodu  ·  July 8, 2026  ·  6 min read

Reasoning models spend thousands of tokens thinking before they answer a single question, and those tokens are the bill. On most serving platforms output tokens cost three to six times what input tokens cost, so the reasoning trace is where the money goes. That has started a search for ways to make the trace shorter without making the model worse.

One proposal looks free. A paper published earlier this year reported that telling a model to reason in a language whose tokenizer packs more meaning per token gives the same accuracy on math benchmarks for 20 to 40 percent fewer tokens. No fine-tuning, no distillation, one line in the system prompt.

I wanted to know whether that holds outside the conditions that produced it. So the study leaned on the two things that most often break a clean result, hard problems in place of saturated benchmarks, and a fixed token budget in place of unlimited generation. It does not hold. And the largest effect in the results table had nothing to do with language.

The setup

Four open-weight reasoning models, Nemotron 3 Ultra, Gemma 4, GLM-5.2, and Mistral Small 4. Five instructed reasoning languages, English, Spanish, Hindi, Chinese, and Arabic. The benchmark questions stay in English and are never translated. Only the system prompt changes, and it tells the model which language to think in. The final answer always comes back in English so grading is identical across cells. Everything runs on BIG-Bench Extra Hard at temperature 0, three runs per cell, about 900 calls in total.

One design choice mattered more than any other. Before comparing efficiency, I ran a language detector over every raw trace to check whether the model had reasoned in the language it was told to. A cell where I asked for Hindi and the model reasoned in English is not a measurement of Hindi efficiency. It is English reasoning under a Hindi label, and reporting it as anything else is a category error. Low-compliance cells are flagged and excluded from the efficiency comparison.

On BBEH that check fails across most of the grid.

Finding 1. Compliance collapses on hard problems

On a standard benchmark the directives work. With in-language instructions, a one-shot example, and an explicit "if you slip into English, switch back" clause, median in-language compliance across the non-English cells was 81 percent on BIG-Bench Hard. On BIG-Bench Extra Hard, with the same directives, the same models, and the same prompts, it fell to 36 percent. Three of the four models revert to English on hard items regardless of instruction. Only Mistral Small 4 keeps any non-English cell above the trust threshold.

I hand-checked the flagged traces in case the detector was confused. It was not. A trace that was supposed to be in Spanish would open with "We are given a question..." and continue in fluent English to the end. I do not know the mechanism. The obvious story is that the instruction loses to whatever the model is strongest at once the problem gets hard enough, but nothing in this study tests that, and I would not want to see it cited as if it did.

One caveat I owe the reader. Harder problems produce much longer traces, and a trace-level compliance measure will look worse as traces get longer even at a fixed per-sentence slip rate. Difficulty and length are tangled together in this number and I have not separated them. The direction is not in doubt, and the practical consequence stands regardless. You cannot claim a cross-lingual efficiency win in a cell where the model never reasoned cross-lingually.

Finding 2. The scoring policy picks the winner

This is the part I did not expect to be writing about. When a response runs out of its token budget mid-thought, you have to decide what to do with it, and that decision changes which model wins.

Count a truncated response as a wrong answer at full cost, which is the deployment-honest reading since you paid for the tokens and got nothing back, and Gemma 4 anchors the accuracy-versus-cost frontier at 69 percent accuracy for about 9,300 tokens a question. Every GLM and Nemotron cell is both less accurate and 1.9 to 3.7 times more expensive per question.

Score only the responses that finished, and GLM-5.2 becomes the most accurate system in the study, at 85 percent in English. It costs 3.7 times as many tokens to get there, and inside a fixed budget it frequently does not finish. Both statements are true. They answer different questions, and they produce opposite rankings from the same 900 calls.

A reasoning-model leaderboard that does not state its truncation policy is not telling you enough to act on. The largest single effect in my main results table is not a model or a language. It is which of these two policies I picked.

Two things I would now insist on in any reasoning-model evaluation. Gate on whether the model did what it was told before trusting a cell, and state the truncation policy in the same table as the numbers.

What I believe now

On saturated benchmarks cross-lingual reasoning looks like a free lunch. On hard verbal reasoning under a budget I found none. The models that could comply mostly stopped complying, the one that kept complying was the weakest of the four, and the efficiency differences that survived were set by my own scoring policy rather than by language.

The limits are real. This is 15 items per cell on a single benchmark family, so only the large gaps are statistically solid and the fine-grained language orderings are not. The compliance collapse confounds difficulty with trace length. Neither changes the headline. Both would change the size of the numbers I would defend.
